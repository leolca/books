Popcorn in the Tidying Labyrinth

Toy cars, modeling clay, scattered socks, books everywhere...  
The chaos that fuels creativity can also consume —  
and trap you in a labyrinth.

Things go missing, monsters appear —  
and even the superhero loses his cape!

In this story, we follow Popcorn as he discovers  
that tidying up isn’t punishment,  
but a way to prepare for even greater flights.

Because growing up also means learning to organize the world —  
inside and out —  
without ever giving up on dreaming and flying.  
But not too high... so you don’t get burned.

Preface:  
Popcorn, like every child, lives between chaos and care,  
between fertile imagination and the boundaries of drawers.

Reading this story with a child means diving headfirst into that universe where,  
between missing things, surprises, and mess,  
we discover the value of care —  
and the beauty of order that arises without losing the magic of imagination.

May this book be a chance to create memories,  
to laugh, talk, and fly together with joy and affection,  
finding, page by page, the delicate balance between order and disorder.

Pages 26 and 27 (Afterword):

In Greek mythology, Daedalus was a brilliant architect, the creator of the famous labyrinth of Crete. Trapped inside his own creation, he built wings of feathers and wax to escape with his son, Icarus. Daedalus represents order, technical knowledge, and practical wisdom. But Icarus, thrilled by the flight, ignored his father's advice, flew too high... and the sun melted his wings.

This story is a parable about the balance between order and chaos, freedom and limits, creativity and safety.

The labyrinth, after all, is an ingenious construction — planned with order and precision. Yet those who enter it can become lost, and things disappear. In the same way, a child's room may seem chaotic, but it's full of meaning for the one who lives there. And even tidying up, if too rigid, can become a maze of rules where creativity hides or gets lost.

It's important to remember: messes have their place too. Chaos, in childhood, is fertile ground for invention, stories, and discovery. Putting away toys is a gesture of care — not repression. Organizing is preparing the space to fly better, not clipping wings (or capes).

The aim of this book isn’t to teach rigid rules, but to celebrate the delicate balance between the creative freedom of childhood and the gentle cultivation of responsibility.

Because growing up is, after all, learning how to fly... without forgetting not to go too high.
